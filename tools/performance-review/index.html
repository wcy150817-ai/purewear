Conducting a fair, defensible staff performance review in a commercial janitorial company, residential maid service, hospital environmental services (EVS) department, or campus facilities team requires more than a subjective 1-to-5 star check. When supervisors rely on vague impressions like "cleans well" or "needs to work faster," employees receive no actionable coaching on how to qualify for a merit raise, and operations managers lack objective documentation if a client complains about missed restrooms or dull corridor floors.

According to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) Occupational Outlook Handbook for SOC 37-2011 (Janitors and Cleaners, Except Maids and Housekeeping Cleaners), core building-cleaning duties encompass sweeping, mopping, scrubbing, and vacuuming floors; washing walls, glass, windows, and partitions; emptying trash receptacles and removing waste; cleaning and restocking restrooms; performing minor building upkeep; and locking doors to secure facilities after hours. Because wages across this occupation span a wide distribution—with BLS reporting a median hourly wage of approximately $17.71 per hour ($36,840 annually), the lowest 10 percent earning around $13.77 per hour ($28,640 annually), and the highest 10 percent earning around $24.17 per hour ($50,270 annually)—tying wage progression to an objective, weighted appraisal rubric gives technicians a transparent path from entry-level probation to senior lead pay tiers.

1. Weighted Custodial Performance Scoring & Merit Raise Equations

An unweighted appraisal treats every habit as equally important—meaning a technician who is polite to coworkers (5/5) and arrives on time (5/5) could still pass an unweighted review even if their restroom sanitization and floor care (2/5) trigger client cancellation notices or OSHA chemical citations. By applying explicit competency weights that total 1.00 (100%), facility managers ensure that core cleaning quality and regulatory safety carry decisive mathematical authority.

2. Aligning Work Quality (35%) With APPA Cleanliness Levels 1–5

The single biggest source of friction during a cleaning staff performance review is a disagreement over what "clean" looks like. Facility directors and university operations teams solve this by anchoring Pillar 1 (Work Quality, 35% weight) to the five APPA Levels of Cleanliness defined by APPA (Leadership in Educational Facilities) in its Operational Guidelines for Educational Facilities: Custodial.

Importantly, an APPA audit score must be evaluated relative to the target APPA level funded by the client's Service Level Agreement (SLA) and square-footage staffing allocation. A custodian assigned 35,000 square feet per shift under a budget designed for APPA Level 3 (Casual Inattention) cannot fairly be penalized for failing to deliver APPA Level 1 (Orderly Spotlessness) showpiece floors. Instead, the supervisor grades whether the technician consistently achieves the assigned target appearance standard across their zone.

3. Evaluating OSHA 29 CFR 1910.1200 & 1910.132 Safety Compliance (20% Weight)

In commercial janitorial, medical facility, and hospitality operations, a technician's safety score (Pillar 3, 20% weight) is not just an operational preference—it is a federal compliance check. Two intersecting Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) standards govern day-to-day custodial work: 29 CFR 1910.1200 (Hazard Communication / HazCom) and 29 CFR 1910.132 (Personal Protective Equipment / PPE).

Under 29 CFR 1910.1200, employers must maintain accessible Safety Data Sheets (SDSs) for every concentrated disinfectant, floor stripper, acid bowl cleaner, and degreaser in the janitor's closet, and must ensure that every secondary spray bottle or mop bucket is properly labeled with product identity and hazard warnings. Section 8 of each chemical's SDS specifies the exact exposure controls and personal protective equipment required, which feeds directly into the employer's certified workplace hazard assessment mandated by 29 CFR 1910.132(d).

Furthermore, under OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132(f), every custodial worker must be trained—and must demonstrate an understanding of that training prior to performing work—across five mandatory PPE elements, documented via a written certification record showing the employee's name, training date, and subject covered:

  • 1. When PPE is necessary: Recognizing exact task triggers, such as dispensing concentrated quaternary ammonium or sodium hypochlorite solutions, stripping floor finish, cleaning restrooms, or handling trash liners that may contain sharps.
  • 2. What PPE is necessary: Selecting the right barrier for the chemical hazard—understanding why thin disposable nitrile gloves suffice for neutral daily cleaners, whereas chemical-splash goggles, aprons, and heavy butyl or neoprene gloves are required when handling corrosive floor strippers or acid descalers.
  • 3. How to properly don, doff, adjust, and wear PPE: Removing contaminated gloves without transferring chemical or biological residue onto bare wrists, and adjusting splash goggles over prescription eyewear.
  • 4. The limitations of the PPE: Knowing that standard surgical or dust masks do not protect against volatile chemical vapors or chlorine off-gassing, and recognizing chemical permeation limits.
  • 5. Proper care, maintenance, useful life, and disposal: Rinsing and air-drying reusable face shields and chemical boots, inspecting HEPA vacuum cords and ground pins for fraying before plugging into wet-area GFCI outlets, and discarding torn single-use gloves immediately.

When scoring Pillar 3 during a quarterly appraisal, supervisors should verify both daily behavioral habits (zero unlabeled spray bottles on the cart, never mixing bleach with ammonia or acid bowl cleaners, proper wet-floor sign placement) and whether the employee's written 29 CFR 1910.132(f) training certification is up to date in their personnel file.

💡 Worked Case Study (Illustrative Example): 90-Day Appraisal vs. Turnover Replacement Math
1
Technician Baseline (BLS Median Benchmark): Suppose a commercial cleaning technician currently earning the BLS SOC 37-2011 median wage of $17.71/hr (working 40 hours/week, or 2,080 hours/year = $36,836.80/year) completes their 90-day appraisal.
2
Weighted Scorecard Calculation: The supervisor scores the technician at SQ = 4.5 (Quality, 35%), SA = 5.0 (Attendance, 25%), SS = 4.0 (OSHA Safety, 20%), and ST = 4.5 (Teamwork & Lock-Up, 20%). Using the weighted equation:
Scoreweighted = (0.35 × 4.5) + (0.25 × 5.0) + (0.20 × 4.0) + (0.20 × 4.5) = 1.575 + 1.250 + 0.800 + 0.900 = 4.525 ≈ 4.53 / 5.00.
Converting to the 100-point index: Index100 = (4.525 ÷ 5.00) × 100 = 90.5 / 100 (Tier 1: Outstanding).
3
Merit Step Cost Calculation: Under an illustrative 4.0% merit step policy for 90+ index scores, the technician's hourly rate increases to $17.71 × 1.04 = $18.42/hr (+$0.71/hr). Across 2,080 annual hours, this merit increase costs the company $1,473.47 per year ($122.79/month).
4
Comparing Merit Cost Against Turnover Replacement Cost: In an illustrative replacement scenario where losing a trained cleaner requires $350 in job-board ads and background screening, 24 hours of supervisor ride-along training at $26.00/hr ($624.00), 40 hours of dual-staffed onboarding at $17.71/hr ($708.40), and $400 in uniform issue and initial client rework credits, a single turnover event costs $2,082.40—exceeding the entire full-year cost of a 4.0% merit raise ($1,473.47) by $608.93 while preserving APPA Level 2 account stability.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you calculate a weighted staff performance review score for cleaning technicians?
To calculate an objective weighted staff performance review score, assign each core job competency a percentage weight that sums to 100 percent—such as 35 percent for Cleaning Quality and APPA Inspection Standards, 25 percent for Attendance and Punctuality, 20 percent for OSHA Chemical and PPE Safety Compliance, and 20 percent for Client Communication and Teamwork. Multiply the technician's 1-to-5 rating in each pillar by its decimal weight, sum the four weighted values to obtain a composite score out of 5.00, and multiply by 20 to express the final appraisal on a 100-point index.
How do the five APPA cleanliness levels help remove subjectivity from custodial staff reviews?
Published by APPA (Leadership in Educational Facilities) in its Operational Guidelines for Educational Facilities, the five APPA levels define visual appearance standards from Level 1 (Orderly Spotlessness) and Level 2 (Ordinary Tidiness, the standard benchmark for commercial and educational campuses) down to Level 3 (Casual Inattention), Level 4 (Moderate Dinginess), and Level 5 (Unkempt Neglect). Tying a cleaner's work-quality rating directly to room audit scores against the target APPA level assigned in the building's Service Level Agreement replaces vague supervisor opinions with observable physical criteria.
What OSHA safety requirements should be evaluated during a janitorial performance appraisal?
Custodial safety evaluations should verify compliance with OSHA 29 CFR 1910.1200 (Hazard Communication) and 29 CFR 1910.132 (Personal Protective Equipment). Specifically, supervisors should evaluate whether the technician maintains readable secondary chemical container labels, follows Safety Data Sheet (SDS) dilution instructions, and demonstrates the five mandatory PPE elements under 29 CFR 1910.132(f): knowing when PPE is necessary, what PPE is required, how to properly don and doff gear, understanding PPE limitations, and performing proper care, maintenance, and disposal.
What is the typical hourly wage range for janitors and building cleaners in the United States?
According to U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) Occupational Outlook Handbook data for SOC 37-2011 (Janitors and Cleaners, Except Maids and Housekeeping Cleaners), the median hourly wage is approximately $17.71 per hour ($36,840 annually), with the lowest 10 percent earning around $13.77 per hour ($28,640 annually) and the highest 10 percent earning around $24.17 per hour ($50,270 annually). Structuring merit wage step increases around documented 30-day, 90-day, and semi-annual performance scorecards helps cleaning businesses retain skilled lead technicians.
