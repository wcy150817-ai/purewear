3. The 2024–2026 DOL Economic Reality Rule (29 CFR Part 795) & State ABC Tests

Simply having a cleaner sign an "Independent Contractor Agreement" does not make them a legal 1099 subcontractor. Federal and state auditors evaluate the actual day-to-day relationship under two primary legal frameworks:

A. TheStrict State "ABC Test" (CA, MA, NJ, IL, WA & 20+ States for Unemployment)

In an ABC-test jurisdiction, every worker is presumed to be a W-2 employee unless the cleaning company proves all three prongs:

  • Prong A (Free from Control): The cleaner is free from direction and control in how they perform the cleaning, both under contract and in fact.
  • Prong B (Outside Usual Course of Business — The Maid Service Killer): The work performed is outside the usual course of the hiring entity's business. If your company advertises "Residential House Cleaning" and hires a solo cleaner to clean houses, you automatically fail Prong B in strict ABC states like California and Massachusetts—unless you operate as a pure software marketplace/referral agency under specific statutory exemptions (e.g., CA Business & Professions Code § 17550 / Civil Code domestic referral agency rules).
  • Prong C (Independently Established Trade): The subcontractor is customarily engaged in an independently established cleaning business of the same nature (has their own LLC/EIN, General Liability policy, business bank account, and cleans for multiple unrelated clients).

B. The Federal DOL 6-Factor Economic Reality Test (29 CFR Part 795) & IRS Common-Law Control

Effective March 2024 and enforced throughout 2026, the U.S. Department of Labor evaluates six totality-of-the-circumstances factors: (1) Opportunity for profit or loss depending on managerial skill, (2) Investments by the worker and potential employer (who buys the $800 HEPA backpack vacuum and commercial floor scrubber?), (3) Degree of permanence of the working relationship, (4) Nature and degree of control over scheduling, pricing, and supervision, (5) Extent to which the work performed is an integral part of the employer's business, and (6) Skill and initiative.

4. What Happens in an IRS or Workers' Comp Misclassification Audit? (IRC Section 3509 Math)

Suppose a residential cleaning service pays 5 full-time cleaners $36,000 each per year ($180,000 total 1099-NEC payroll) without collecting Certificates of Workers' Compensation Insurance. Two years later, one cleaner injures their lower back lifting a vacuum or files for state unemployment after being let go. That single claim triggers a cross-agency data match between the state unemployment agency, the Workers' Compensation board, and the IRS:

  • IRS Section 3509(a) Reduced Withholding Assessment (When 1099-NEC Forms Were Filed): The IRS assesses 100% of employer FICA (7.65%), plus 20% of employee FICA (1.53%), plus 1.50% for federal income tax withholding = 10.68% of gross payouts ($19,224 per year audited).
  • IRS Section 3509(b) Penalty Rate (When 1099-NEC Forms Were NOT Filed): The employee FICA share doubles to 40% (3.06%) and income tax withholding doubles to 3.00% = 13.71% of gross payouts ($24,678 per year) plus $310+ per missing Form 1099-NEC.
  • Retroactive Workers' Comp Audit (NCCI Class 9014 @ $4.85/$100): Your insurance auditor adds all $180,000 of uninsured subcontractor payouts to your policy audit, generating an immediate $8,730 retroactive premium bill.
  • FLSA Unpaid Overtime & Drive-Time Back Wages: If those 1099 cleaners drove between 4 houses a day and worked 44 hours/week (including unpaid windshield time), the DOL can assess 2 to 3 years of back overtime plus 100% liquidated (double) damages under 29 U.S.C. § 216(b).

6. Frequently Asked Questions (1099 vs. W-2 Cleaning Labor & Tax Rules)

How much more does a W-2 cleaning employee cost than their base hourly wage in 2026?

A W-2 residential or commercial cleaner typically adds 18% to 28% in mandatory statutory payroll burden and operational fringe costs above their base hourly rate. This includes 7.65% employer FICA (6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare), 0.6% net FUTA ($42/year on the first $7,000), 1.5% to 5.4% state SUTA unemployment tax, and 3.5% to 7.8% in NCCI Class Code 9014 Janitorial Workers' Compensation insurance, plus paid drive time and IRS mileage reimbursement.

Can a residential maid service legally pay cleaners on a 1099-NEC in ABC-test states?

In strict ABC-test states (such as California under AB5, Massachusetts, and New Jersey), a residential cleaning company generally fails Prong B because house cleaning is the exact core business of the hiring entity. Even in states using the IRS common-law control test or the DOL 2024 Economic Reality Rule (29 CFR Part 795), requiring cleaners to wear company uniforms, follow a proprietary 50-point checklist, use company chemicals, or work fixed daily dispatch schedules makes them W-2 employees by law.

What are the IRS penalties for misclassifying W-2 cleaners as 1099 subcontractors under IRC Section 3509?

Under Internal Revenue Code Section 3509(a), if the IRS audits a cleaning business and reclassifies 1099 workers as W-2 employees (when Form 1099-NEC was timely filed), the employer is assessed 100% of the employer FICA share (7.65%), plus 20% of the employee's FICA share (1.53%), plus 1.5% of total wages for unwithheld federal income tax—totaling 10.68% of gross wages before interest, FUTA, and state SUTA/Workers' Comp back-audits. If Form 1099-NEC was not filed (IRC § 3509(b)), the assessment rises to 13.71% plus information-return penalties.

Why do insurance carriers charge a Workers' Comp premium for uninsured 1099 janitorial subcontractors?

During an annual Workers' Compensation payroll audit, your insurer will inspect all Form 1099-NEC payouts. If a 1099 cleaning crew cannot provide a valid Certificate of Insurance (COI) showing active Workers' Compensation coverage—or an approved state corporate officer exemption—the insurance auditor automatically adds 100% of that subcontractor's labor payouts into your NCCI Class 9014 payroll base and bills you the full W-2 Workers' Comp rate retroactively.
