Square Foot vs. Hourly Rate: How to Quote Cleaning Jobs Profitably (2026 Calculator & Guide)

One of the most consequential decisions cleaning business owners face when submitting bids is whether to price work by the square foot or by the cleaner hour. Choosing the wrong bidding structure on a commercial facility or residential deep clean leads directly to margin compression, uncompensated scope creep, or lost contracts to sharper competitors.

The Fundamental Conflict: Efficiency vs. Time Spent

In commercial cleaning and residential maid services, every billing methodology incentivizes a specific operational behavior. When you bill by the hour, your gross revenue is tied directly to clock time. If your cleaners complete a job in 2 hours instead of 3, your revenue drops by 33% even though the client received identical or better results. Hourly pricing fundamentally penalizes operational investments such as high-velocity microfiber flat mops, backpack HEPA vacuums, and standardized chemical dispensing stations.

Conversely, when you quote by the square foot or convert square footage production metrics into a fixed flat-rate monthly retainer, your revenue is tied to space, cleanliness standards, and deliverables. If your crew optimizes their pathing and completes a 10,000-square-foot office in 3.0 labor hours instead of 4.2 labor hours, your gross revenue remains constant while your effective hourly yield jumps from $35.71 to $50.00 per hour.

However, square-foot quoting carries significant risks if applied blindly. If a facility has heavy soil loading, irregular architectural obstructions, or erratic occupant usage (such as a medical urgent care center or automotive showroom), standard square-footage pricing will severely underestimate labor requirements, converting a seemingly profitable job into a financial drain.

The Missing Link: ISSA Production Rates (Sq Ft Per Hour)

Experienced commercial cleaning contractors do not guess square foot prices from thin air. They derive their square foot pricing directly from production rates—the number of square feet a trained technician can thoroughly clean in 60 minutes.

According to standardized time-and-motion studies published by ISSA (The Worldwide Cleaning Industry Association), production rates vary widely based on facility layout, flooring composition, and fixture density:

  • Open Commercial Office (Carpeted): 2,800 to 4,200 sq ft/hour. Involves trash pulling, spot vacuuming traffic lanes, and surface wiping.
  • Dense Partitioned Cubicles: 2,000 to 2,600 sq ft/hour. Individual trash cans, tight turns, and chair maneuvering slow technician velocity.
  • Medical Facilities & Dental Clinics: 1,200 to 1,600 sq ft/hour. Stringent terminal cleaning protocols, dwell-time requirements for EPA-registered disinfectants, and bio-waste compliance.
  • High-Traffic Restrooms: 700 to 1,200 sq ft/hour. Deep fixture sanitation, tile scrubbing, mirror polishing, and stock replenishment.
  • Industrial Warehouses (Open Concourse): 4,000 to 7,500 sq ft/hour. Sweeping and automatic scrubber operation across expansive open concrete.
  • Residential Maintenance Clean: 1,400 to 1,800 sq ft/hour. Multiple living zones, personal items, kitchens, and showers.
  • Post-Construction / Move-Out Deep Clean: 500 to 900 sq ft/hour. Scraping adhesives, interior cabinetry detailing, baseboards, and window tracks.

The Mathematical Bidding Formula

Labor Hours = Total Square Feet ÷ Realistic Production Rate
Base Labor Cost = Labor Hours × Fully Burdened Hourly Wage
Job Operating Cost = Base Labor Cost + Consumables (3%) + Indirect Overhead (18%)
Target Bid Price = Job Operating Cost ÷ (1 − Target Profit Margin %)

Calculating Fully Burdened Labor: Why $18/hr Really Costs $23.40/hr

The most common reason cleaning companies underbid contracts is relying on nominal payroll wages rather than fully burdened labor costs. If you pay an employee $18.00 per hour on payroll, your actual cash outlay per hour is substantially higher due to mandatory statutory employer contributions and direct operational insurance:

  1. Federal FICA (Social Security & Medicare): 7.65% fixed employer match ($1.38/hr).
  2. Federal & State Unemployment Insurance (FUTA/SUTA): Typically 2.5% to 4.5% depending on state experience rating (~$0.55/hr).
  3. Workers' Compensation Insurance: For commercial janitorial classifications (e.g., Code 9014), rates generally run between 3.5% and 8.0% of gross payroll depending on claims history (~$0.95/hr).
  4. Non-Productive Paid Time (Drive Time & Breaks): Paid travel between daytime job sites or statutory 10-minute rest breaks adds an effective 5% to 8% overhead (~$1.00/hr).
  5. General Liability Insurance Allocation: Approximately 2.0% allocated across direct labor hours (~$0.36/hr).

In total, an $18.00/hr cleaner costs your cleaning business approximately $22.24 to $23.40 per billable hour before factoring in equipment, administrative rent, software, or profit. When submitting hourly bids, billing below $40.00 to $45.00 per labor hour virtually guarantees thin margins or outright operational losses once vehicle fuel and supervisory overhead are counted.

The Winning Industry Compromise: The Hybrid Flat-Rate Quote

Elite commercial cleaning operators rarely pitch client-facing quotes as raw hourly rates, nor do they rely on simple arbitrary square-footage rules of thumb. Instead, they utilize a Hybrid Flat-Rate Quoting Architecture:

Phase 1: Internal Hourly Calculation

Measure square footage, calculate task-by-task production hours, add a 15% safety buffer for unpredictable delays, and multiply by fully burdened hourly rate plus target net margin.

Phase 2: Square Footage Sanity Check

Divide total bid price by square footage to ensure the resulting rate falls within regional competitive bands ($0.09–$0.15/sq ft for daily, $0.15–$0.24 for weekly commercial).

Phase 3: Client-Facing Fixed Proposal

Present the quote to the building manager as a single, all-inclusive monthly retainer specifying exact frequency, scope of work, and quality guarantees. Never expose labor hours to client scrutiny.

This hybrid structure gives your client what they value most—cost certainty and predictable invoicing—while protecting your business from the efficiency trap of hourly billing.

5 Cost Drivers Cleaners Frequently Forget in Bids

A bidding formula is only as sound as the expense ledger supporting it. If you fail to account for non-labor direct expenses, your calculated 25% net profit margin will quickly vanish into administrative deficits:

  1. Consumable Chemical Concentrates and Paper Supplies: Commercial janitorial operations typically consume between 2.0% and 4.0% of gross contract value in multi-surface disinfectants, floor finishes, glass cleaners, and trash liners. In contracts where you provide paper towels and hand soap, supply costs easily escalate to 8% to 12% of billing.
  2. Drive Time, Fuel & Vehicle Depreciation: For mobile residential teams or multi-facility commercial routes, non-billable windshield travel time constitutes between 12% and 18% of employee paid hours. This must be absorbed into your hourly labor burden or charged as a direct trip fee.
  3. Equipment Amortization & Maintenance: Commercial equipment such as floor burnishers, commercial extractors, and HEPA backpack vacs have a 2- to 4-year service life. Factor $0.75 to $1.50 per operational hour for machine depreciation, belt replacements, and filter renewals.
  4. Quality Audits and Re-Cleans: Plan for a 1.5% to 3.0% reserve for supervisory callbacks, touch-up visits, and quarterly deep-scrub remediations.
  5. Uncollected Receivables & Net-30 Payment Terms: Commercial clients frequently pay on Net-30 or Net-60 day cycles. Ensure you have cash-flow reserves or build a 1.5% financing buffer into extended payment terms.

Step-by-Step Walkthrough: Conducting a Site Walk & Submitting a Bid

When a prospective commercial client requests a quote, follow this battle-tested site walk protocol to assemble an airtight, profitable proposal:

1. Complete a Quantitative Room-by-Room Audit

Do not rely on the client's stated square footage. Measure total usable area with a digital laser distance measure. Break down square footage into distinct floor types: carpeted offices, VCT / vinyl tile, polished concrete, and ceramic restroom tile. Note the number of restroom stalls, kitchen breakroom sinks, and trash receptacles.

2. Establish Service Frequency & Cleaning Window

Determine whether cleaning occurs during daytime business hours or after-hours lockup. Night janitorial offers higher production speeds (15% faster due to zero employee obstruction), whereas daytime portering requires continuous interruptions and lower production rates.

3. Run the Production Hours Formula

Apply ISSA standards to each zone. For example: 8,000 sq ft office / 3,000 = 2.67 hrs; 4 restrooms / 20 mins each = 1.33 hrs. Total labor budget = 4.0 labor hours per visit.

4. Add Burdened Labor, Overhead, and Profit Margin

Multiply 4.0 hours by your fully burdened labor cost ($23.00/hr = $92.00 direct labor). Add 3% supplies ($6.00) and 18% overhead ($25.00). Total job cost = $123.00. For a 25% target net margin, divide $123.00 by 0.75 = $164.00 per visit.

5. Format Proposal with Explicit Scope & Exclusions

Present your bid as a monthly contract ($164.00 × 52 weeks ÷ 12 months = $710.67/month for 1x/week service). Attach an exact scope of work checklist. Clearly list exclusions (exterior high-rise glass, biohazard remediation, floor stripping) with scheduled hourly pricing for supplementary work orders.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Should you quote commercial cleaning by the square foot or by the hour?

For recurring commercial office and facility cleaning contracts, quoting by the square foot or as a monthly flat-rate based on square footage production rates is strongly recommended. Commercial clients budget in square feet ($0.09 to $0.22/sq ft depending on cleaning frequency and facility type), and square footage pricing rewards your operational efficiency as your crew becomes faster, whereas hourly billing penalizes speed.

When is hourly pricing better than square footage pricing?

Hourly pricing is superior for residential initial deep cleans, move-in/move-out cleanings, post-construction cleanup, and hoard or high-clutter environments. In these jobs, the dirt density, grease buildup, and customer expectations vary wildly and do not correlate with floor area, exposing contractors to severe losses under flat square footage rates.

What is standard janitorial production rate per hour?

According to ISSA (The Worldwide Cleaning Industry Association) standards, average production rates for standard office spaces range from 2,500 to 4,000 square feet per cleaner per hour for routine vacuuming, dusting, and trash removal. Medical clinics and high-density restrooms drop to 1,000 to 1,800 square feet per hour due to stringent disinfection protocols.

How do you calculate your fully burdened labor cost for cleaning quotes?

To calculate fully burdened labor cost, take your technician base hourly wage and multiply by 1.18 to 1.25 (an 18% to 25% burden). This burden covers employer-paid FICA (7.65%), state and federal unemployment taxes (SUTA/FUTA, typically 2.5% to 4.5%), workers' compensation insurance (3.5% to 8.0%), and general liability insurance allocations.
