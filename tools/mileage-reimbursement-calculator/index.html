3. IRS Publication 463: What Counts as Reimbursable Cleaning Mileage?

One of the most frequent payroll disputes in residential and commercial cleaning is whether a technician should be reimbursed for driving from their house to the first job of the morning. Under IRS Publication 463 (Travel, Gift, and Car Expenses) and the FLSA Portal-to-Portal Act (29 CFR § 785.35), the boundary between personal commuting and business mileage depends entirely on where the technician starts and ends their workday:

  • Scenario A — Direct Home-to-First-Client Dispatch (Solo Techs Keeping Supplies in Trunk): Driving from the cleaner's home to Client House #1, and driving from Client House #4 back home at 4:30 PM, is classified by the IRS and Department of Labor as ordinary personal commuting (not reimbursable and not paid drive time). However, every single mile driven from Client #1 to Client #2, Client #2 to Client #3, and Client #3 to Client #4 is 100% deductible business mileage and compensable work time.
  • Scenario B — Morning Equipment Depot / Office Check-In (Crew Model): If your company requires technicians to arrive at your central office or chemical locker at 7:30 AM to pick up clean microfiber towels, dilute disinfectants (see our HOCl & Bleach Dilution Calculator), and load vacuums, the workday officially starts at 7:30 AM at the office. Every mile driven from the office to Client #1, between all clients, and from the last client back to the office to unload dirty laundry is 100% reimbursable business travel and paid hourly work time!

4. FLSA "Travel All in a Day's Work" (29 CFR § 785.38) & Minimum Wage Protection

Many cleaning owners mistakenly believe that paying $0.70 per mile under the IRS standard rate relieves them from paying an hourly wage while technicians are driving between houses. Under federal 29 CFR § 785.38 ("Travel that is all in a day's work"), time spent by an employee traveling from one job site to another during the workday must be counted as hours worked.

You may legally establish a separate, lower "Travel Rate / Windshield Wage" (for example, $12.00/hr or state minimum wage for drive time vs. $19.00/hr for active scrubbing time), provided:

  1. The employee is notified of the dual-rate pay structure in writing before performing the work;
  2. The travel hourly rate is at least equal to the highest applicable federal, state, or city minimum wage; and
  3. When total weekly hours (active cleaning hours + inter-site driving hours) exceed 40 hours in a workweek, FLSA overtime is calculated using the weighted average regular rate of both pay rates (see our Hourly Pay & Overtime Wage Calculator).

Furthermore, under the FLSA "Free and Clear" rule (29 CFR § 531.35), if an employee earns close to the minimum wage and is forced to use their own vehicle to drive 35 miles a day between houses without reimbursement, those out-of-pocket fuel and maintenance costs are treated by the Department of Labor as an illegal kickback to the employer if they reduce the cleaner's net hourly earnings below minimum wage. In states such as California (Labor Code § 2802), Illinois (820 ILCS 115/9.5), and Massachusetts, employers must reimburse 100% of necessary business mileage regardless of how high the cleaner's hourly wage is.

6. Frequently Asked Questions (Cleaning Fleet Mileage & IRS Compliance)

What is the IRS standard mileage reimbursement rate for cleaning businesses in 2025 and 2026?

Under IRS Notice 2025-5, the federal optional standard business mileage rate is $0.70 per mile (70 cents/mile, including a $0.33/mile depreciation component), up from $0.67 per mile in 2024. Cleaning business owners can use this rate under an IRC Section 62(c) Accountable Plan to reimburse W-2 technicians tax-free for driving between client homes or commercial job sites.

Do cleaning companies have to pay for drive from home to the first cleaning job?

Under IRS Publication 463 and the FLSA Portal-to-Portal Act (29 CFR § 785.35), ordinary commuting from a technician's home directly to the first client site and from the last client site back home is non-deductible personal commuting and non-compensable travel time. However, if you require cleaners to report to your central office or chemical warehouse first to load supplies and equipment, the trip from the office to the first job site (and all travel between client sites during the day under 29 CFR § 785.38) is 100% compensable work time and reimbursable business mileage.

Why is a flat monthly car stipend worse than an IRS Accountable Plan for maid services?

If you pay a cleaning supervisor or solo tech a flat $300/month car allowance without requiring contemporary odometer/GPS logs under IRC Section 62(c), the IRS classifies it as a Non-Accountable Plan. The entire $300 must be added to Box 1 W-2 taxable wages, costing your company an extra 7.65% in employer FICA taxes plus Workers' Comp premiums, while the employee loses 15% to 29% of the allowance to income and FICA withholding.

When does FLSA require mandatory vehicle reimbursement for cleaning employees?

Under FLSA 29 CFR § 531.35 ("Free and Clear" minimum wage rule), unreimbursed business expenses—such as a technician buying fuel and putting wear-and-tear on their personal car to drive between four residential houses a day—cannot push the worker's net effective hourly wage below the federal, state, or municipal minimum wage. Additionally, states such as California (Labor Code § 2802), Illinois, and Massachusetts mandate 100% reimbursement of necessary employee vehicle expenses regardless of wage level.
