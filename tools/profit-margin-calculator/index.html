Ask ten cleaning company owners why cash feels tight despite $25,000 or $50,000 in monthly gross billings, and nine of the answers trace back to a single ratio: loaded payroll as a percentage of gross revenue. In both residential house cleaning and commercial office janitorial, field labor is your largest Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) line item. When direct technician wages, employer payroll taxes, Workers' Compensation, and unbillable inter-job drive time creep past 52% to 55% of revenue, net operating profit gets wiped out even when your schedule is completely booked.

Core Custodial Profit & Payroll Ratio Formulas:
• Base Wage % of Revenue = (Direct Cleaner Gross Wages ÷ Monthly Gross Revenue) × 100
• Loaded Field Payroll ($) = Direct Cleaner Gross Wages × (1 + Employer Tax & Workers' Comp Burden %)
• Loaded Payroll % of Revenue = (Loaded Field Payroll ÷ Monthly Gross Revenue) × 100
• Gross Profit Margin (%) = ((Gross Revenue − [Loaded Field Payroll + Direct Supplies]) ÷ Gross Revenue) × 100
• Net Operating Margin (%) = ((Gross Revenue − Total COGS − Fixed Overhead) ÷ Gross Revenue) × 100

House Cleaning & Commercial Janitorial Payroll Percentage of Revenue Benchmarks

When benchmarking house cleaning business payroll percentage of revenue against commercial cleaning company payroll ratios, never mix base hourly wages with fully loaded payroll, and never lump office dispatchers into field COGS. Under IRS Publication 15 (Circular E), employers owe 7.65% FICA (6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare) plus 0.6% net FUTA (after the standard 5.4% state credit on the first $7,000 of annual wages per employee), before adding State Unemployment (SUTA) and custodial Workers' Compensation insurance (often classified under NCCI Code 9014 or 0917). Combined, mandatory employer burden typically adds 14% to 22% on top of base hourly pay—see our 1099 vs W-2 Cleaning Labor Cost & Tax Calculator to model your state's exact rate.

Step-by-Step Cleaning Company Profit Tracking System

Many small operators check their business bank balance on Friday afternoon and assume whatever remains after payroll is profit—only to get blindsided by quarterly tax deposits, annual general-liability renewals, or a three-paycheck calendar month. Reliable cleaning company profit tracking relies on four monthly habits:

  1. Separate Field COGS from Overhead in Your Chart of Accounts: Put cleaner hourly wages, paid inter-site travel time (required for W-2 employees under FLSA 29 CFR 785.38), employer payroll taxes, Workers' Comp, and diluted chemicals inside Cost of Goods Sold. Keep office admin pay, software, marketing, and insurance inside Operating Overhead. Use our Janitorial Chart of Accounts & Sanitary Supply Expense Ratio Calculator and Cleaning Income & Expense Ledger to structure every category cleanly.
  2. Track Realized Dollars per Cleaner Man-Hour Weekly: Divide gross job revenue by total clocked technician hours (including setup and drive time between houses). If your target loaded bill rate is $52 per man-hour and a bi-weekly house yields only $34 per man-hour due to clutter creep, either recalibrate scope with our Residential & Commercial Cleaning Quote Calculator or send a formal rate-adjustment notice.
  3. Isolate Customer Tips from Wage Ratios: Client gratuities passed through to cleaners are not company revenue and cannot subsidize minimum-wage obligations unless tip-credit rules apply—split crew tips accurately with our Maid Service Tip Pooling Calculator so payroll percentages stay clean.
  4. Budget on Accrual Rather Than Bi-Weekly Cash Spikes: Two months every year contain three bi-weekly payroll runs instead of two. Converting bi-weekly payroll to a monthly accrual (`Bi-Weekly Payroll × 26 ÷ 12`) prevents artificial profit swings on your monthly P&L.

4 Silent Leaks That Push Payroll Percentage Past 52%

  • 1. Unbillable Windshield Time: Two cleaners spending 45 minutes driving between three scattered zip codes burn 1.5 paid man-hours producing $0 in billable revenue, plus IRS mileage reimbursement ($0.70/mile benchmark—see our Cleaning Vehicle Mileage Calculator). Tight geographic route clustering typically cuts payroll ratio by 4% to 7%.
  • 2. Under-Priced Commercial Square-Foot Bids: Bidding commercial offices at a flat $0.08/sq ft without counting restroom fixtures or trash pulls forces crews over budget. Verify man-hours first with our Commercial Janitorial Bidding Calculator and ISSA 612 Cleaning Time Estimator.
  • 3. Re-Cleans & Unstructured Callback Labor: Every unpaid quality callback doubles direct labor on that ticket, pushing a 42% payroll job to 84% instantly.
  • 4. Free-Pour Chemical Waste: Mixing disinfectant concentrates at 1:16 instead of 1:64 or 1:128 quadruples chemical COGS—audit bottle costs in our Disinfectant Oz-Per-Gallon Dilution Calculator.
Minimum Billable Man-Hour Rate Formula (Illustrative Example):
Target Hourly Bill Rate = (Loaded Hourly Wage + Hourly Supply Allowance) ÷ (1 − [Overhead % + Target Net Margin %])

Illustrative Calculation: If base cleaner pay is $19.00/hr with an 18% employer tax/Workers' Comp burden ($22.42/hr loaded) plus $1.30/hr in supplies, and you target 18% fixed overhead and 20% net operating profit:
• Minimum Billable Rate = ($22.42 + $1.30) ÷ (1 − 0.38) = $23.72 ÷ 0.62 = $38.26 per clocked man-hour (or ~$45.00/hr on-site after allowing 15% route travel time).

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the benchmark payroll percentage of revenue for a house cleaning business?

For W-2 residential maid services, direct cleaner base wages typically target 32% to 39% of gross revenue, while fully loaded field payroll (including employer FICA at 7.65%, FUTA, SUTA, and Workers' Compensation insurance) should stay between 38% and 48% of gross revenue. Adding 6% to 10% for office or dispatcher wages brings total company-wide payroll to roughly 46% to 55% of revenue.

How does commercial janitorial payroll percentage differ from residential maid services?

Commercial office janitorial contracts typically run a higher direct loaded field payroll ratio—around 45% to 55% of revenue—because evening routes have minimal inter-site drive time, lower marketing churn, and larger monthly invoice sizes, allowing healthy 12% to 18% net operating profit even with tighter gross margins.

What is a normal net profit margin for a cleaning company?

Recurring residential cleaning companies commonly target 15% to 24% net operating profit after administrative overhead, whereas multi-crew commercial janitorial contractors typically realize 12% to 18% net profit and specialty post-construction or restorative floor-care jobs can reach 30% to 45% net margin per project.

How do you track cleaning company profit accurately every month?

Accurate cleaning company profit tracking separates Cost of Goods Sold (direct technician wages, employer payroll taxes, Workers' Comp, paid drive time, and diluted chemical usage) from fixed Operating Overhead (office/admin salaries, CRM software, general liability insurance, fleet leases, and client acquisition marketing), reconciled monthly on an accrual P&L.

Why does quoting only base hourly wage ruin cleaning profit margins?

Under IRS Publication 15 rules, every W-2 dollar incurs 7.65% employer FICA (6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare) plus FUTA (0.6% net on the first $7,000), state unemployment (SUTA), and custodial Workers' Compensation—typically adding 14% to 22% above base hourly pay before paid travel time is even counted.
