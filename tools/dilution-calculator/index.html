The 4-Step Professional Dilution Blueprint

⚠️ The Golden Custodial Rule: "Always Add Chemical to Water, Never Water to Chemical"
Pouring high-pressure water directly into concentrated chemical causes foaming overflow, acid splashing, and aerosolized vapors. Always fill your bottle with water first, then add the measured concentrate.
Universal Dilution Mathematics:
Total Volume Parts = Dilution Ratio Number (X) + 1 Part Concentrate
Chemical Concentrate Volume = Total Solution Volume ÷ (X + 1)
Water Volume = Total Solution Volume − Chemical Concentrate Volume
1. Verify SDS & Target Ratio

Check chemical manufacturer's Technical Data Sheet. Over-concentrating leaves sticky residue that attracts dirt.

2. Measure Cold Water First

Fill bottle 85% full with cold tap water. Avoid hot water as it vaporizes active disinfectants.

3. Add Measured Concentrate

Pour the exact concentrate calculated above into the water. Invert bottle gently twice to mix without foaming.

4. Affix OSHA Secondary Label

OSHA mandates secondary container labeling showing product name and GHS pictograms for worker safety.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Why must I always add water before adding chemical concentrate?
The universal standard for custodial chemical safety is "Always Add Chemical to Water". Pouring water directly onto pure concentrated acid or disinfectant causes sudden chemical splashing and violent bubbling that can cause immediate skin burns or eye injuries. Filling your spray bottle or mop bucket with water first safely diffuses the concentrate as it is poured.
What is the difference between 1:64 and 1:128 dilution ratios?
A 1:64 dilution yields 2 ounces of chemical concentrate per gallon of water (1.5% solution) and is standard for heavy hospital-grade disinfecting against pathogens like Norovirus, MRSA, and influenza. A 1:128 dilution yields 1 ounce per gallon (0.78% solution) and is formulated for neutral floor cleaners and streak-free window washing where heavy chemical residue would otherwise leave sticky streaks.
Can I use warm or hot water to dilute cleaning disinfectants?
No. Unless explicitly specified by the chemical manufacturer, always dilute disinfectants and bleach with cold or room-temperature water (under 85°F / 30°C). Hot water accelerates chemical evaporation, deactivates active chlorine and quat compounds, and releases dangerous volatile chemical vapors into the air.
What are OSHA requirements for secondary spray bottles?
Under OSHA Hazard Communication Standard (29 CFR 1910.1200), any secondary bottle or bucket containing diluted chemicals must have a GHS-compliant label stating the chemical product identity, manufacturer, and key hazard warnings unless used immediately and entirely by the person who filled it.
